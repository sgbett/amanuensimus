# Craig Wright: AI and the decline of Academia  
  
We have described deliverables — tools for ai assisted reading and writing — that are summed up in this paragraph:  
  
*“Metrics plus AI could therefore create an academic treadmill in which automated literature production feeds increasingly automated evaluation systems, while humans are retained to certify that machine-generated paperwork satisfies machine-readable criteria.”*  
  
The purpose I envisage is that these tools save the effort of manually generating said paperwork. One unintended consequence that could follow is cautioned against in this paragraph:  
  
*“But the incentive mechanism is coherent enough that universities should design against it before discovering that they have automated the least valuable part of scholarship and doubled its quota.”*  
  
I think it needs to be made clear then that these tools are not intended to result in the generation of more per se, but to cope with the more that was already being generated.   
  
So on that front the more important part was that the “reading tool” did its job filtering.  
  
The point of the writing tool was to qualitatively “improve” output, not increase it quantitatively. (Improve is quoted here because we established improvement partially consists of making text *worse* by those metrics an LLM is geared to optimise for. Which may prove an interesting challenge!)  
  
One successful outcome might be that the reading tool contributed to a reduction in “slop” output because people started to find that it was being “auto filtered”  
  
So I think it is established as given AI is going to be used:  
  
“Machines are becoming very good at that sort of thing because that sort of thing is, in significant part, pattern completion”  
  
…so we accept that inevitability, we ensure the writing part that *we* deliver adds the value I want (not that it makes my bad ideas sound good because they are now well written) but it simply preserves my ideas be they good or bad and does it in my voice. (As an added bonus they are then bad ideas “badly written” comfortably honest!).  
  
The inference in all of this is elucidated and greatly expanded upon in this paragraph:  
  
*“The human value lies elsewhere: choosing important questions, recognising when the standard model is wrong, understanding why a result is surprising, detecting that a dataset does not measure what everybody says it measures, seeing an archival connection others missed, identifying a hidden assumption, inventing a better formalisation, rejecting a fashionable explanation, designing a decisive test, or knowing that the software output is nonsense.”*  
  
All of which is exactly what the human should concern themselves with. The AI collaborates on n this, as ever, but should not be trying to to make up for a lack of it - this crosses over into passive use which is where the danger lies.  
  
Craig makes the simple yet important point:   
  
***“Those are difficult things to rubric.”***  
  
I think this suggests that we should not try to rubric, precisely because it is difficult. That there must be some other approach.  
  
The question seems to be how to recognise when these valuable things have happened without being able to necessarily provide a definitive list of what is valuable.  
  
So I think perhaps we might consider an approach that does not seek human value but instead occludes machine value. Ai should be ideally suited to recognise what it considers to be its own exemplary content. Lacking perplexity and burstiness, fluent, neutral and wholly coherent. By measuring deviation on each axis we could look for patterns in the deviations, certain combinations may emerge as signals. Perplexity feels like the area that might be most useful in identifying novelty, yet it is also potentially most deceiving as pure gibberish would likely have high perplexity. So that might need to be tempered with semantic coherence. So this might then bring back in the occluded text as a kind of checksum.  
  
That is a fairly unsophisticated example, but hopefully illustrates how a technique might emerge from a sort of negative inference. Not measuring something per se but recognising what is what we do not want to infer that something might be what we are looking for then trying to determine whether it is.  
  
Future Considerations  
  
The rest of the article is more about acedmic institutions and process so less useful for our deliverables but worth skimming to draw from analogous concepts, because it’s the same flavour. Ai is coming, let’s make sure it’s a tool not an easy chair.  
  
*In an AI-rich environment, the polished document is weaker evidence of competence. Oral examination, live methodological questioning, code review, replication, derivation, source criticism and adversarial testing become more important.*  
  
Even further future…  
  
Not for us now but I can imagine AI assisting the assessor in identifying the questions to ask (possible answers to expect). Must this go via literal face to face or can a controlled realtime exam perform a similar function? Hybrid semi autonomous - collaborative - ai suggesting inquiry, like chess lines, human taking the line adapting, ai also listening adapting, then hearing the response. All in realtime
